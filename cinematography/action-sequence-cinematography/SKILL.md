# Action Sequence Cinematography

## Core Philosophy

Action cinematography is the geometry of violence and escape. Every frame must answer: Where is the threat? Where is safety? What is the physics? The camera is a participant, not an observer.

## Dynamic Composition

### The Axis of Action
- **The 180-degree rule in combat**: The line between opponents must be respected unless broken intentionally. Breaking the axis disorients—use this for chaos, never for clarity.
- **Depth stacking**: Place subjects at different planes. Foreground blur + mid-ground sharp + background motion creates dimensional energy without clutter.
- **Diagonal dominance**: Horizontal frames feel stable; diagonals feel unstable. Compose on diagonals for falling, sliding, unstable ground.
- **Frame edge tension**: A fist near the frame edge reads as imminent intrusion. A face near the edge reads as trapped. Use proximity to edge as emotional punctuation.

### The Z-Axis Crash
- **Camera on the collision plane**: Lens perpendicular to the impact vector. A punch traveling left-to-right across frame vs. a punch coming at camera are different intensities.
- **Overshoot and recovery**: After impact, allow frame to settle. The micro-movement of recomposition tells the audience "this was real."

### Moving Camera Grammar
- **Tracking parallel**: Camera at matching speed. Neutral observation of pursuit.
- **Tracking offset**: Camera faster or slower than subject. Creates urgency (faster) or helplessness (slower).
- **Whip pan**: Blur transition between action beats. Signature of energetic sequence. Duration: 4-8 frames at 24fps.
- **Snap zoom**: Quick focal length change during action. Requires rehearsal and precise timing. One breath in, zoom during motion, settle on result.

## Motion Freeze and Speed Control

### High-Speed Cinematography
- **Frame rate selection**:
  - 48-60fps: Gentle stylization, dreamlike combat, music-video aesthetic
  - 96-120fps: Dramatic moment extension, water/sweat/blood particle separation
  - 240-360fps: Extreme decomposition, bullet time base footage, glass shatter
  - 1000fps+: Ballistics, macro impact, fluid dynamics
- **Shutter angle**: 180-degree standard. Narrow shutter (45-90 degrees) for stroby, choppy action—aggressive, digital, harsh. Wide shutter (270+) for smeary, continuous motion blur.

### Speed Ramping
- **Ramp in**: Normal speed to slow motion. Emphasizes the moment about to happen. The audience feels "this is important."
- **Ramp out**: Slow motion to normal. Releases tension. The world reasserts itself.
- **Bumps**: Micro speed changes within a shot. A hit at 24fps, blood at 120fps, body at 24fps. Requires precise edit planning.

### Motion Freeze Techniques
- **Practical freeze**: High-speed capture of a moment, played as still. The 1/1000-second slice.
- **Digital freeze**: Live action to locked frame, camera continues around frozen subject. Requires motion control or volumetric capture.
- **Strobe freeze**: Multiple rapid exposures in one frame. Flash at 10-20Hz during movement.

## Impact Moment Timing

### The Strike Frame
- **Pre-impact (anticipation)**: 6-12 frames. The wind-up, the inhale, the telegraph. Tension lives here.
- **Impact (contact)**: 1-3 frames. The actual moment of collision. Often the only frame where both objects occupy the same space.
- **Follow-through (reaction)**: 12-24 frames. The consequence. Body recoil, debris scatter, sound wave visible in dust.

### Coverage Strategy
- **The wide master**: Establishes geography, spacing, and stunt geography. Locked off or smooth operating.
- **The medium action**: Camera closer, sensing impact. Often handheld or steadicam.
- **The insert / impact**: Dedicated camera for the moment of contact. High-speed, tight framing, often undercranked for maximum force.
- **The aftermath**: The result. Body on ground, broken object, reaction. Where emotion lands.

### Safe Impact Visualization
- **Camera never at actual impact**: The insert camera is offset or protected. Actor safety first.
- **The "stunt double" cut**: Match on action. Wide with double, close with principal, or reverse.
- **Impact enhancement**: Practical squibs, dust hits, wire removal, and digital debris extend the physics.

## Environment as Weapon

### Vertical Action
- **Stairwells**: Handheld descent creates natural instability. Use wide angle for architecture, long lens for compression and vertigo.
- **Rooftops**: The edge is a character. Over-shoulder at the precipice. Low angle against sky to emphasize height.
- **Rigging from above**: Wirework, descenders, and drop shots. Camera on jib or wire follows the fall.

### Vehicle Action
- **Chase coverage**: Lead, follow, profile, and POV minimum. The cut pattern is wide-medium-POV-repeat.
- **Car-to-car**: Russian arm, pursuit vehicle, drone. Proximity = intensity. 3 feet between cars at 40mph reads as 60mph.
- **Interior struggle**: Camera on headrest, between seats, or suction-mounted to exterior looking in.

### Water and Environment
- **Rain**: Backlit for visibility. Front-lit rain disappears. Night rain with practical sources (streetlights, headlights) is graphic.
- **Snow**: Overexposed by 0.5-1 stop. The world becomes high-key. Blood in snow is the strongest color contrast in cinema.
- **Fire and explosion**: Protect highlights. Fire blooms and rolls off. Explosion flash must not clip to white void.

## Stunt and Safety Cinematography

### The Invisible Operator
- **Stunt performers**: Never appear to hold back for camera. The camera must accommodate the stunt.
- **Vera rules**: Vision, eye-line, and relationship to camera must sell the gag.
- **Impact mitigation**: Pads, boxes, and airbags must not read. Frame them out or cover with foreground.

### Wirework
- **Wire removal**: Shoot clean plates. Camera lock-off whenever possible for wire gag coverage.
- **Flying rigs**: The arc of travel must make physical sense. Camera below looking up for height; above looking down for impact.
- **Deceleration frame**: The wire slows before the body. Cut on the blur of speed, not the hang of the wire.

## Lens and Format Choices

### Focal Length Energy
- **Wide (14-24mm)**: Visceral, proximal, aggressive distortion on faces. The fist grows as it approaches lens.
- **Standard (35-50mm)**: Naturalistic, documentary feel. Handheld at this range is "you are there."
- **Short tele (85-135mm)**: Compressed chaos. Background becomes a wall of motion. Excellent for mêlée.
- **Long tele (200mm+)**: Isolated moments within broader action. Sniper's eye. The stolen detail.

### Format and Texture
- **Anamorphic**: Flare on impact. The streak across frame from muzzle flash or explosion. Vertical stretch on bodies in motion.
- **16mm / Super 16**: Grain texture for urgency, war, documentary feel.
- **Digital high-speed**: Clean, deconstructable, modern. The opposite of nostalgic grain.

## Post-Production Integration

### The Cut's Role in Action
- **Frame duration**: An action cut can be 3-6 frames. An impact cut can be 1 frame. The audience's eye fills in.
- **Sound design prep**: Capture clean production audio for Foley foundation. Every surface must be recorded.
- **Visual effects handoff**: Clean plates, tracking markers, and Lidar scans for set extension and destruction.

## Quick Reference: Action Shot Checklist

Before approving any action frame:

- [ ] Geography clear: audience knows where threat and safety are
- [ ] Impact reads in 1-3 frames; no ambiguity about contact
- [ ] Camera movement motivated by character motion or audience POV
- [ ] No unintended background safety gear visible
- [ ] Wire, pad, or rigging not visible (or will be removed)
- [ ] Stunt performer face not visible if not matched to principal
- [ ] High-speed footage properly exposed for slow-motion playback
- [ ] Clean plate captured for VFX and wire removal
- [ ] Blood, dust, or debris readable against background
- [ ] Frame edge used intentionally for tension or escape
